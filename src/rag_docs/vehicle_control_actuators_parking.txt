VEHICLE CONTROL ACTUATORS — PARKING-SPECIFIC FAILURE MODES

Purpose:
- Document actuator subsystem specifications and failure modes specific to
  automated parking assist (APA) and automated valet parking (AVP) features.
- Provide concrete component names, operating parameters, and documented
  failure mechanisms for HAZOP cause and effect generation.


=== ELECTRIC POWER STEERING (EPS) ===

Architecture: column-mounted or rack-mounted BLDC/PMSM motor drives a
reduction gear to apply steering assist torque. Torque sensor on input shaft
measures driver input. ECU computes assist based on vehicle speed, driver
torque, and steering angle. Typical parking assist torque range 5-14 Nm at
the motor (50-140 Nm at the rack through reduction gear ratio 10:1).

Key components: BLDC motor, MOSFET H-bridge inverter (6 switches), torque
sensor (magnetostrictive or strain gauge), steering angle sensor (absolute
encoder), rack position sensor.

Failure modes:

  MOSFET bridge failure: a single MOSFET short-circuit in the H-bridge causes
  uncontrolled current flow through one motor phase. Depending on which switch
  fails, this produces either a constant assist torque in one direction (pull
  to left or right) or complete loss of assist. MOSFET failure rate increases
  with junction temperature — thermal derating begins above 120C, with
  accelerated failure above 150C.

  Torque sensor drift: over time and temperature cycling, the torque sensor
  zero-point drifts by 0.1-0.5 Nm. The EPS ECU interprets this offset as
  constant driver input, producing a persistent assist bias. At parking speed
  where nominal driver torque is 2-5 Nm, a 0.5 Nm offset represents 10-25%
  error in the assist calculation.

  Full-lock saturation at parking speed: during tight parking maneuvers, the
  steering system reaches mechanical end-stop. The EPS motor stalls against
  the end-stop, drawing maximum current (30-50 A). If the driver or automated
  system continues to command beyond end-stop, the motor draws continuous stall
  current. Thermal protection limits motor-on time to 5-15 seconds at stall
  before triggering thermal shutdown.

  Thermal derating: sustained full-lock maneuvers (common in tight parking)
  cause motor and MOSFET temperatures to rise. Above 100C motor winding
  temperature, the ECU progressively reduces maximum current — assist torque
  drops by 30-50%. This manifests as gradually increasing steering effort
  during multi-point parking maneuvers.

  Steering angle sensor absolute position loss: if the sensor loses its
  absolute reference (power cycle during motion, battery disconnect), the ECU
  cannot determine true wheel angle until a learning drive is completed.
  During this period, the APA system cannot execute angle-precise maneuvers.


=== INTEGRATED ELECTRONIC BRAKE (IEB) / BRAKE-BY-WIRE ===

Architecture: electromechanical unit replaces traditional vacuum booster. An
electric motor drives a ball screw to generate brake master cylinder pressure.
Typical maximum pressure 180-200 bar. Pressure build time from zero to full:
<150 ms (typical target). Suppliers include HL Mando (iMEB), Continental
(MK C2), Bosch (iBooster + ESP hev).

Key components: brushless DC motor, ball screw mechanism, master cylinder
with dual-circuit hydraulic output, pressure sensor, motor position encoder,
ECU with dual-core safety processor.

Failure modes:

  Insufficient pressure at repeated low-speed stops: during automated parking,
  the brake system is commanded to make frequent stops at very low speed
  (<5 km/h). The motor repeatedly cycles between zero and low pressure (10-30
  bar). Under certain conditions, the ball screw mechanism does not advance
  fully, producing pressure 20-40% below commanded value. This failure mode
  was documented in the Kia EV9 NHTSA recall (24V-693, January 2024) affecting
  HL Mando IEB units — repeated stops at speeds below approximately 2 mph
  produced insufficient braking pressure.

  Motor thermal limitation: continuous low-pressure cycling (as in stop-and-go
  parking maneuvers) heats the motor windings. Above thermal threshold, the
  ECU limits maximum motor current, reducing peak available braking pressure
  from 200 bar to approximately 120 bar. This degradation may not trigger a
  brake warning lamp if the pressure remains above the minimum threshold.

  Ball screw backlash: mechanical wear in the ball screw assembly introduces
  0.1-0.3 mm of backlash. When the ECU reverses motor direction (switching
  from brake apply to brake release), the backlash creates a dead zone —
  the motor must travel through the backlash before pressure changes. This
  adds 20-40 ms to pressure response, which can be significant during
  emergency brake requests at close range.

  Hydraulic circuit imbalance: the dual-circuit master cylinder feeds front
  and rear axle circuits. If one circuit has a slight restriction (air bubble,
  partially blocked line), pressure builds unevenly. The vehicle decelerates
  asymmetrically, pulling to one side during braking — particularly noticeable
  at the low speeds used in parking.

  Pressure sensor offset: the internal pressure sensor develops a zero-offset
  drift over time. The ECU's closed-loop pressure control compensates, but if
  the offset exceeds the compensation range (typically +/-5 bar), the actual
  pressure deviates from commanded pressure without triggering a fault code.


=== ELECTRIC DRIVE (TRACTION MOTOR) ===

Architecture: for battery-electric vehicles used in AVP, the traction motor
provides propulsion and regenerative braking. The inverter converts DC battery
voltage to three-phase AC. A creep function simulates automatic-transmission
idle creep at 3-5 km/h for parking maneuvers.

Failure modes:

  Creep function calibration error: the creep torque target is calibrated for
  flat-ground operation. On parking structure ramps (8-16% gradient), the
  calibrated creep torque may be insufficient to hold the vehicle, causing
  rollback. Conversely, on steep downhill grades, creep torque may be excessive,
  causing unintended acceleration toward the parking space.

  Inverter gate driver failure: a single gate driver IC failure in the three-
  phase inverter causes loss of one motor phase. The motor produces pulsating
  torque with 33% reduced average output and significant vibration. The vehicle
  can still move but with jerky, unpredictable motion.

  Regenerative braking calibration error: during parking, the vehicle
  alternates between motoring and regenerative braking. If the regen torque map
  is miscalibrated, the vehicle decelerates too aggressively (jerky motion,
  uncomfortable for passengers, imprecise position control) or too weakly
  (relies on friction brakes for all deceleration, increasing brake wear and
  response time).

  Parking pawl engagement failure: at the end of a parking maneuver, the
  transmission controller commands the parking pawl to engage. If the pawl
  does not fully engage (misalignment, worn detent, insufficient actuator
  force), the vehicle is not mechanically locked. Combined with a brake
  release, the vehicle may roll freely.


=== ELECTRIC PARKING BRAKE (EPB) ===

Architecture: cable-actuated or caliper-integrated electric motor applies
parking brake to rear wheels. Engagement force typically 15-30 kN at the
caliper. Auto-hold function applies EPB automatically when the vehicle stops.

Failure modes:

  Cable stretch: over time and repeated actuation, the steel cable connecting
  the EPB motor to the rear calipers stretches. This reduces clamping force
  at the caliper. On inclines exceeding 10%, the reduced clamping force may
  be insufficient to hold the vehicle — the vehicle creeps or rolls slowly
  downhill.

  Auto-hold release logic error: the EPB auto-hold function releases the brake
  when the driver (or APA system) commands drive torque. If the release
  threshold is set too low, the EPB releases before sufficient drive torque is
  established, causing momentary rollback on inclines. If set too high, the
  EPB remains engaged while the traction motor drives against it, causing
  excessive current draw and potential drivetrain damage.

  Caliper-integrated EPB motor seizure: the small DC motor inside the caliper
  assembly is exposed to heat (brake rotor temperature can exceed 200C during
  repeated braking) and moisture. Corrosion or thermal damage to the motor
  bearings can cause seizure — the parking brake remains applied and cannot be
  released electrically, requiring manual release.


=== COMMUNICATION NETWORKS (CAN / CAN-FD / ETHERNET) ===

Architecture: parking-related ECUs communicate over CAN (500 kbaud) or CAN-FD
(2-5 Mbaud) buses. Some newer architectures use automotive Ethernet (100BASE-T1
or 1000BASE-T1) for high-bandwidth sensor data. A central gateway routes
messages between domains (powertrain, chassis, body, ADAS).

Failure modes:

  Bus-off state: CAN protocol forces a node into bus-off state after
  accumulating 256 error counts (transmit error counter). A faulty node
  generating errors can drive other nodes to bus-off. In bus-off, the ECU
  cannot transmit or receive messages. Recovery requires bus-off recovery
  sequence (128 x 11 recessive bits), taking 1.3-25 ms depending on
  implementation.

  CRC failure and frame discard: CAN-FD frames include a CRC field. If
  electromagnetic interference (from ignition, EPS motor, or external sources)
  corrupts a frame, the CRC check fails and the frame is discarded. The
  receiving ECU uses the last-known value for that signal. For safety-critical
  signals (wheel speed, steering angle), stale data persists until the next
  valid frame, typically 10-20 ms.

  Arbitration delay under high bus load: CAN uses priority-based arbitration.
  Low-priority messages (parking sensor data, typically 0x600+ message IDs)
  are delayed when higher-priority messages (powertrain, ABS, typically
  0x100-0x200) occupy the bus. At bus utilization above 80%, low-priority
  parking messages experience delays of 10-50 ms beyond their nominal period.

  Gateway routing error: the central gateway routes messages between bus
  segments. A gateway firmware fault or configuration error can mis-route,
  delay, or drop messages. If a brake command from the parking ECU is routed
  to the wrong bus segment, the brake controller never receives it.

  Message authentication failure: SecOC (Secure On-Board Communication) adds
  authentication to safety messages. If the authentication key or counter
  desynchronizes between sender and receiver (e.g., after ECU reset), valid
  messages are rejected as inauthentic. The receiving ECU treats the signal
  as unavailable.

  Ethernet switch latency: automotive Ethernet switches introduce 5-50
  microsecond forwarding latency per hop. Under heavy traffic (camera streams,
  LiDAR point clouds), queuing delays can increase to 1-5 ms. For time-
  critical sensor data, this added latency affects fusion timestamp accuracy.
